# OPERATION MIDNIGHT SUN
## Documentary production script and edit blueprint

**Target running time:** 32:00. **Format:** 16:9, 4K master, 24 fps. **Cut:** 16 two-minute sequences. **Voice:** calm, intimate, measured; original performance, not an imitation of any particular narrator. **Research snapshot:** October 30, 2026. **Status:** pre-production script, not a rendered video.

**Timing:** Each sequence is allotted 2:00, including its specified picture-only passage. Aim for an unhurried delivery around 105–125 words per minute, varying naturally with the scene. Record a scratch narration and adjust the lengths of the picture-only passages to land exactly at 32:00; the timestamps are edit targets, not a claim that the narration has already been recorded and timed.

**Editorial rules:** All violence and intent described below are attributed to the relevant allegation or plea. California defendants are presumed innocent. No narration invents dialogue, motives, or the contents of unseen recordings. Recreated scenes are composites, not literal depictions of known people's appearances or precise actions. Do not show recipes, assembly, tactical instructions, actual target identities, or readable operational material. No on-screen text, captions, title cards, labels, logos, or readable documents; provide accessible captions as a separate optional track. All dates and sources belong in the video description, not over the image. No real surveillance footage, photos, music, or interview audio without confirming rights. A musical score must be original or licensed. Avoid creating sound bites that impersonate real people.

### 00:00–02:00 | THE TENT
**Picture:** An aerial glide over a stark desert at first light; sand-blown grasses trace the foreground. A tiny white tent sits within an enormous empty landscape. Cut to an abstract, low-poly table: indistinct objects in shadow, no readable labels. A distant aircraft is only a silhouette. Never show assembly. The people are anonymous geometric figures, not portraits. Keep a visual hold of approximately 20 seconds, distributed across the section.

**Sound:** Dry wind and an almost inaudible low drone. A bright, distant high note emerges when the aircraft appears. Strip the music back to air before the arrest. No gunshots or invented shouted commands.

**Narrator:**

December twelfth, 2025. Somewhere in California's Mojave Desert, four people gather beneath a tent. According to federal prosecutors, they have come to prepare and test explosives.

They are not alone.

An FBI confidential source has been part of the investigation. An undercover employee has joined the group. And above the desert, a surveillance aircraft is watching.

Before the group can complete a functional device, agents move in. Four arrests. No explosion. No attack on New Year's Eve. [1, 2, 3]

The authorities say they stopped a coordinated bombing plot targeting businesses across Southern California. The plan, they say, had a name: Operation Midnight Sun.

But the story doesn't end with the arrests. Because the same undercover investigation that made the case possible also leaves a question hanging over it.

How much did the people in that tent bring with them? And how much, if anything, did the government bring into the room?

This is an account of the public record—not a verdict. Some allegations are contested. Some evidence has not been made public. And those differences matter.

### 02:00–04:00 | EIGHT PAGES
**Picture:** Eight blank, off-white sheets lift and turn in an impossible black space, their edges catching a cool light. They align into a single plane, then separate into five pools of light that reveal anonymous warehouse silhouettes. No text, handwriting, symbols, maps of actual facilities, or instructions. Approximately 15–20 seconds of picture-only music, distributed.

**Sound:** Felted piano notes spaced far apart; a pulse enters as the sheets separate. Brief silence after “eight pages.”

**Narrator:**

According to the Justice Department, the investigation centered on a handwritten document. Eight pages.

Prosecutors say Audrey Carroll drafted it in November and gave it to others, including a person who was secretly a paid FBI source. They say it described plans to attack five or more locations belonging to two companies in the greater Los Angeles area. [1, 2, 4]

The targets, authorities said, were technology and logistics businesses. The companies were not identified in the reporting we reviewed. These were not empty symbols in the abstract: businesses have workers, visitors, security personnel, and neighbors. The real people in and around a building are part of what makes the accusation serious.

The timing matters too. Investigators say the proposed attacks were to occur at midnight on New Year's Eve. Reporting based on the affidavit says fireworks were expected to mask the sound. [4]

None of that proves a completed attack. None occurred. The document is evidence the government says it obtained; its meaning, context, and authorship are matters for the legal process. And to understand that process, we have to go back to the group in which the plan allegedly circulated.

### 04:00–06:00 | THE NAME AND THE NETWORK
**Picture:** A city at dusk becomes a constellation of distant windows; a handful brighten, then connect without drawing an operational network diagram. At ground level, ordinary commuters continue past closed shopfronts. Alternate amber city lights with cold interiors. Approximately 20 seconds of image-only storytelling.

**Sound:** Subdued city ambience, lightly textured synth and bowed strings, no recognizable protest chants.

**Narrator:**

The Turtle Island Liberation Front described itself in terms of decolonization and tribal sovereignty. Prosecutors characterized the group as anti-government and anti-capitalist. Within it, according to the government, was a smaller faction Carroll called the Order of the Black Lotus. Members communicated through a private group chat. [1, 2]

The name Turtle Island has a much wider history. Some Indigenous peoples use it for North America, drawing on Indigenous traditions. That history is not the property of this group, and a federal allegation against a few people tells us nothing about Indigenous communities as a whole. [3]

Likewise, support for Palestinian rights, criticism of immigration enforcement, and opposition to capitalism are not the same thing as planning violence. The alleged conduct—not the mere existence of political beliefs—is what prosecutors are required to prove.

That distinction can get lost when a group acquires a name and a headline. A name makes scattered people look like a single, coordinated institution. But the public record tells us far less about the group's size and reach than the story's most dramatic versions suggest.

One much-repeated figure, eighteen YouTube followers, could not be verified from the sources reviewed for this film. So we won't use it as proof of anything.

### 06:00–08:00 | THE PEOPLE ACCUSED
**Picture:** Four separate, unoccupied spaces: a dim apartment table, a parking structure, a bus stop, a computer desk. Do not imply these are actual homes or personal belongings. Slow push-ins; individual spaces retain distinct color palettes. Picture-only hold approximately 20 seconds.

**Sound:** Piano subsides; a single sustained viola note runs under the names. No ominous stings attached to any one defendant.

**Narrator:**

The four California defendants are Audrey Carroll, Zachary Page, Dante Anthony-Gaffield, also referred to in reports as Dante Gaffield, and Tina Lai. Federal prosecutors say Carroll wrote the plan and recruited others, including Page, Anthony-Gaffield, and Lai. That is the prosecution's account, not a finding that every person participated in the same way. [2]

Carroll and Page were charged with conspiracy to use a weapon of mass destruction, as well as material-support and unregistered-firearm offenses. Anthony-Gaffield and Lai faced material-support and unregistered-firearm charges. The statutory maximums announced in December 2025 were life for Carroll and Page and twenty-five years for Anthony-Gaffield and Lai if convicted on the original charges. Maximums are not predictions of actual sentences. [2]

By early January 2026, Carroll, Page, and Lai had pleaded not guilty. [3] A development involving Anthony-Gaffield came much later, and we'll return to it.

A charging document is a statement of what the government intends to prove. It is not a transcript of a trial. And it is not a substitute for hearing what a defense may say about the same events.

### 08:00–10:00 | A SOURCE INSIDE
**Picture:** A geometric meeting room. Five chairs around a table, seen first through opaque glass. One chair is gradually isolated by a moving beam of light; no identifying portrait or fabricated recording transcript. Transition to a notebook closed by an unseen hand. Approximately 20 seconds of picture without narration.

**Sound:** The score narrows to a low pulse and room tone; let the chair reveal occur without an impact sound.

**Narrator:**

At the center of the investigation was a confidential human source. The Intercept reported that the source was paid and had been vetted by the FBI since 2021. That does not mean the source had been inside this group since 2021. How long the source was involved with the group before the alleged plot emerged remains unclear from the reporting we reviewed. [4]

According to the reported affidavit, the source supplied the FBI with the eight-page document, access to the group chat, and a recording of a December second meeting involving Carroll and Page. An undercover FBI employee also attended later meetings. [4, 5]

Those facts are important for two reasons. First, they explain how investigators could know what was being discussed in a private group. Second, they raise questions about what happened before the record visible to the public begins.

Who proposed what? Who encouraged whom? What was the source asked to do, and what did the source actually do?

The answers could support the government's case, the defense's account, or something more complicated. An undisclosed answer is not evidence for either side. But it is still a question worth asking.

### 10:00–12:00 | THE RECORD AND THE GAP
**Picture:** A long corridor of illuminated doorways. Some open on abstract evidence: a closed notebook, a muted phone, an empty chair. Others remain dark. The camera stops before a closed door. Approximately 15–20 seconds of quiet visual space.

**Sound:** Drone recedes until only a faint ventilation hum remains. A single, unresolved chord returns near the end.

**Narrator:**

On December seventh, according to reporting based on the affidavit, Carroll, Page, Anthony-Gaffield, the confidential source, and an undercover employee met. Prosecutors say the participants discussed the proposed New Year's Eve attack and possible later attacks on immigration officers. [2, 4, 5]

The indictment attributes to Carroll a description of proposed violence against officers intended to “take some of them out and scare the rest of them.” That quotation is a prosecution allegation. It is not a clip we can independently authenticate by listening to an original recording here. [2]

The World Socialist Web Site characterized the investigation as a possible case of entrapment and argued that government participants had played a substantial role. It also acknowledged that the affidavit did not establish who first suggested the bombing plot. [5]

Those are different claims. The first is a critical interpretation. The second is a limitation of the available record. Neither, by itself, proves that the government invented the plan.

A gap in evidence can make two explanations possible. It does not make them equally supported. To judge between them, we need the earlier communications, instructions to the source, contemporaneous recordings, and whatever evidence a court ultimately allows the public to see.

### 12:00–14:00 | WHAT ENTRAPMENT MEANS
**Picture:** Two beams cross in a black room; one points toward a seated silhouette, the other toward an open door. The lights brighten separately, never forming words or legal graphics. No gavel imagery or simulated jury verdict. Approximately 15–20 seconds without speech.

**Sound:** Intimate dry piano and warm low strings; no percussion.

**Narrator:**

Entrapment is a legal defense, not simply a synonym for an undercover operation.

In federal law, the Supreme Court has described two related questions: government inducement of the crime, and whether the defendant lacked a predisposition to commit it. Providing an opportunity, using deception, or placing an informant in a group is not automatically entrapment. [6]

The details matter. Did a government actor pressure or persuade someone? Was the person already willing to commit the particular crime before that intervention? What do the communications show? And what might the full recording reveal that a short quotation cannot?

A defendant can contest a charge and also seek an entrapment instruction if the evidence supports it. The legal threshold is for the court to evaluate; factual disputes can go to a jury. [6]

This film has not found a judicial determination that the California defendants were entrapped. Nor have we established that an entrapment defense has been formally raised in their case. Those are separate questions from whether journalists or the public should examine the government's conduct.

Scrutiny is not a verdict. It is a request for the evidence on which one could be based.

### 14:00–16:00 | THE DECISION TO WAIT
**Picture:** A red sun crosses a horizon, then resolves into a sequence of shifting shadows over an empty table. In a separate shot, an anonymous observer watches through a window. Avoid depicting actual surveillance capabilities or operational specifics. Approximately 15–20 seconds of picture-only time.

**Sound:** A low rhythmic pattern builds slowly, interrupted by two beats of near-silence.

**Narrator:**

From late November to December twelfth, the FBI had information from inside the group. The public account describes a written plan, recorded conversations, private messages, and an undercover employee's presence. [1, 4]

Why did agents wait until the desert to make arrests?

There are reasons investigators may continue an operation after learning of a possible crime. They may be trying to identify everyone involved, determine whether the threat is real, preserve evidence, or establish each person's role. An arrest too early can leave important facts unresolved.

There are also risks to waiting. The longer an investigation continues, the more opportunity there may be for dangerous conduct to advance—and the more important it becomes to understand who is influencing it.

We do not have the FBI's full decision log. The public record reviewed here does not establish every opportunity the agency had to intervene, or why it chose this particular moment. Saying “they could have stopped it at any point” would suggest knowledge we do not have.

What we do know is narrower: the arrests came during the desert gathering, and prosecutors say they occurred before a functional device was assembled. [2, 3] The difference between those two statements—the known and the assumed—is where the story should stay honest.

### 16:00–18:00 | BACK TO THE DESERT
**Picture:** Return to the opening landscape, now from ground level. Anonymous figures unload unidentifiable cases; do not show their contents or any construction. A shadow crosses the sand. The tent canvas shifts. On arrest, cut to a distant, wide image; no simulated confrontation. Approximately 20 seconds of visual hold.

**Sound:** Wind, fabric, distant aircraft texture; a slow swell cuts to silence at the moment of intervention. Return with a single low note.

**Narrator:**

December twelfth. The group travels into the Mojave Desert. According to prosecutors, the purpose is to construct and test devices. The confidential source and an undercover employee are with them, according to reporting on the investigation. [1, 4, 5]

The government describes a tent, a table, and materials laid out at the site. We are not showing how any device could have been made. What matters to this story is the allegation that the group moved beyond talk and gathered for a test.

Then the surveillance ends and the intervention begins. Four people are taken into custody. Officials say no functional explosive device had been fully assembled when agents moved in. [2, 3]

The aerial image offers a seductive kind of certainty. From above, everything seems visible: the tent, the vehicles, the people, the empty land around them.

But an image cannot show who first had an idea. It cannot tell us why a participant was there, what had been said before the camera arrived, or how a jury should interpret it. Those answers require evidence beyond the frame.

The investigation also reached beyond California.

### 18:00–20:00 | THE LOUISIANA THREAD
**Picture:** Night driving through a stylized Louisiana landscape, with rain on a windshield and distant headlights. No exact route markings, house, vehicle registration, or tactical details. Cut to an empty passenger seat, then a quiet courthouse corridor. Approximately 15–20 seconds of music and road ambience.

**Sound:** Rain softens into a subdued, low string motif. Keep the pulse restrained.

**Narrator:**

In Louisiana, authorities investigated Micah Legnon, a former Marine and former New Iberia police officer. The Justice Department says Legnon participated in the same chat and was connected to the wider group. [7, 8]

Federal authorities said agents observed Legnon leaving home with weapons and protective equipment in a vehicle. They believed Legnon intended an attack in New Orleans. Legnon was initially charged in connection with threats against immigration officers. The government's December 2025 release says the arrest occurred on December thirteenth, after surveillance on the twelfth; some contemporaneous local reporting gave a different date. [7, 9] We cannot resolve that discrepancy here.

There is a later, important development. On September eighth, 2026, Legnon pleaded guilty in federal court to conspiracy to provide material support to terrorists. The Justice Department said formal acceptance of the plea agreement and sentencing were scheduled for December eleventh, 2026. [8]

That plea is not a conviction of the California defendants, and it does not resolve the question of the informant's role in their case. It is a separate legal proceeding, connected by the government's account of the group and its communications.

### 20:00–22:00 | THE POLITICAL FRAME
**Picture:** Abstract broadcast screens glow in a dark room, each showing only moving color fields—no actual headlines or network marks. Cut between an ICE office exterior represented as a generic federal building and a peaceful crowd of anonymous silhouettes. No suggestion that participants in peaceful protests share the alleged intent.

**Sound:** Fragments of nonverbal radio static, then a low, neutral bed. Twenty seconds of image-only passage.

**Narrator:**

When the arrests became public, federal officials described the alleged plot in the language of domestic terrorism. Attorney General Pam Bondi and other officials announced the case as a major disruption. [1]

The politics were impossible to separate from the coverage. The group expressed anti-government and anti-capitalist beliefs, and prosecutors alleged plans for later violence against immigration officers. At the same time, protests against immigration enforcement and advocacy for Palestinian rights involved many people who had no connection to this case.

The distinction is not a formality. A government has both the responsibility to investigate credible threats and a duty to respect protected speech and political association. Violent conduct can be prosecuted without treating an entire movement as a suspect class.

Reporting by The Intercept and the World Socialist Web Site also placed the case in the context of the administration's 2025 focus on left-wing extremism. [4, 5] That context may help explain public attention and investigative priorities. It does not, on its own, establish that the charges were politically fabricated—or that the allegations are true.

The same caution applies to the online phrase “false flag.” A suspicion can be investigated. Repeating it is not the same as proving it.

### 22:00–24:00 | THE GOVERNMENT'S CASE
**Picture:** The eight blank sheets return, joined by a sealed evidence bag, an empty table, and an abstract visual of a recorded waveform without labels. Each object floats separately, so none appears to authenticate another. Approximately 20 seconds picture-only.

**Sound:** A slightly firmer pulse enters with the first item and fades before the opposing questions.

**Narrator:**

The government's account has several components.

First, prosecutors say Carroll drafted a specific written plan. Second, they say the group discussed targets and later attacks in private communications. Third, they describe efforts to obtain materials and the trip into the desert. And fourth, they say the defendants began preparing for a test before agents intervened. [1, 2]

Taken together, those allegations describe more than political rhetoric. They describe an alleged agreement and steps the government says were taken toward carrying it out.

But each component also has a context. Who was present during the conversations? What came immediately before and after a quote? Who suggested, obtained, or paid for a particular item? Did each defendant know and agree to the same thing?

The importance of those questions does not disappear because an accusation is alarming. It increases. In a criminal case, individual culpability has to be established under the applicable law, not inferred merely from a group name or a photograph of people standing together.

A grand jury issued an indictment. That was a significant legal step. It was not a trial verdict.

### 24:00–26:00 | THE OTHER QUESTIONS
**Picture:** A dark editing suite. The same abstract scene plays forward, then stops before any action completes; a second camera angle reveals only an empty edge of the room. Never imply these are genuine recordings. The screens contain no text. Approximately 15–20 seconds picture-only.

**Sound:** Drop percussion entirely. A soft, dissonant two-note interval resolves only partially.

**Narrator:**

Critics of informant-led investigations ask a different set of questions. What instructions did the source receive? Was the source compensated in ways that could influence their behavior? Did they propose ideas, encourage escalation, or simply report what others were already doing?

The existence of a paid source is established in the reporting. The answers to those more specific questions are not established by that fact alone. [4, 5]

One way to think about it is the missing-angle problem. Imagine a camera pointed at the last scene of a story. It can record what happens in front of it. But if the dispute is about how the people reached that room, the last scene may not be enough.

That metaphor is not evidence of wrongdoing. It is a reason to seek the earlier record: the messages, the source's directions, the recordings, and any material the defense is entitled to examine.

The government can have credible evidence of a dangerous plan. The defense can have legitimate questions about how that evidence was obtained and what it means. Both propositions can be true without deciding the case in advance.

### 26:00–28:00 | THE COURTROOM
**Picture:** An empty courtroom rendered in sculptural, low-poly forms. Morning light travels slowly across separate tables. No faces, seals, writing, or simulated proceedings. Cut to a closed folder and then to its blank reverse side. Picture-only hold approximately 15–20 seconds.

**Sound:** Warm strings replace the darker drone, but do not signal acquittal or conviction. Room tone remains audible.

**Narrator:**

As of our research snapshot on October thirtieth, 2026, the clearest confirmed public account of the California pleas says Carroll, Page, and Lai pleaded not guilty in January. [3] Reporting published October twenty-ninth said a trial for those three had been set for May 2027; schedules can change. [10]

A later report published October twenty-ninth said Anthony-Gaffield had pleaded guilty that day to one count of providing and attempting to provide material support to terrorists. It reported sentencing was scheduled for September fifteenth, 2027. We have not independently inspected the court docket or plea agreement. [10]

Those distinctions matter. A reported guilty plea is not a sentence. A scheduled trial is not a verdict. A statutory maximum is not a sentence. And Legnon's separate guilty plea does not establish the guilt of anyone else.

Before publication, the production team must check the current federal dockets, obtain the relevant filings, and update this sequence. If the status cannot be confirmed, say so. Do not let a beautiful final cut turn an outdated fact into a permanent claim.

### 28:00–30:00 | THE MIRROR IS NOT THE PROOF
**Picture:** Two abstract landscapes face each other across a dark reflective surface: a forest at night, then the Mojave in daylight. The reflection ripples and separates, leaving two distinct frames—not a visual equation. Approximately 15–20 seconds without speech.

**Sound:** The opening motif returns, played by cello rather than piano. It remains unresolved.

**Narrator:**

Other undercover investigations have followed people associated with violent far-right movements. This case involves defendants associated, according to prosecutors, with a group described as far-left. The structural resemblance is striking: private communications, an insider, allegations of violence, and a public announcement after arrests.

But a resemblance is not a finding that the cases are identical. Their histories, sources, evidence, charges, and outcomes can be very different. Nor does the political label tell us whether an investigation was justified or whether a defense will succeed.

The useful comparison is a standard, not a shortcut.

Ask the same questions regardless of the ideology involved. What evidence supports the threat? What did government participants do? Can their conduct be independently examined? What did each defendant actually agree to? What remains unknown?

And apply the same restraint when the answers are incomplete. No one should be convicted by association. No credible threat should be dismissed merely because the accused share someone's politics.

A fair standard is most revealing when it is inconvenient for the side applying it.

### 30:00–32:00 | WHAT THE CAMERA CANNOT SEE
**Picture:** Return to the desert, now in deep blue dusk. The tent is gone. The camera pulls slowly away until the landscape fills the frame. In the final 15–25 seconds, transition from desert to a quiet, ordinary warehouse district at dawn—doors closed, traffic starting, people beginning a day. No title or end card.

**Sound:** The low drone gives way to a fragile, warmer chord; remove all score for a few seconds after the last line. End on natural morning ambience. No sensational explosion.

**Narrator:**

The FBI says it stopped an attack before anyone could carry it out. Its evidence deserves careful examination. So do the actions of the source and the undercover employee whose presence helped produce that evidence.

At the same time, the unresolved question is not permission to fill in the blanks. We do not know, from the sources reviewed here, that the government created the plan. We do not know that it did not influence any part of it. And we do not have a final California verdict to settle the charges.

In the desert, the camera could see a tent and the people gathered around it. It could see agents arrive. But the most consequential part of this story may sit outside the image: earlier conversations, decisions made in private, and evidence that has yet to be tested in court.

The question is not whether we can choose the most satisfying ending.

It's whether we can wait for the evidence—even when the story seems to demand one now.

## Sound, visual, and delivery notes

- **Voice direction:** conversational, close-miked, even dynamics; let facts do the work. Pauses after revelations should feel like thought, not theatrical shock. Never fabricate interview audio. Use distinct environmental beds per location and a continuous musical arc, punctuated by intentional silence.
- **Visual quality:** build a small original visual language rather than imitating a particular creator: faceted, seamlessly modeled figures; restrained film grain; controlled focus; slow dolly, overhead and eye-level shots. Alternate color-rich city passages with desaturated desert and intimate monochrome legal sequences. Avoid rapid flashes. Use procedural transitions only when motivated by the story.
- **No overlays:** render images without embedded titles, captions, legible handwriting, real license plates, brand names, charts, or UI text. Supply optional closed captions as a separate track and a full accessible transcript. In the video description include sources, research date, legal status and a conspicuous note that some shots are reconstructions.
- **Mix:** narration always intelligible above music and ambience; duck music under speech, avoid jarring loudness jumps, and check a final stereo loudness/true-peak master. Use only cleared or original audio.
- **Continuity/QC:** previsualize every scene; check framing at common aspect ratios, color consistency, object intersections, animation artifacts and timeline gaps. No real individuals' likenesses without appropriate rights review. Never present a reconstruction as archival footage.
- **Do not publish without updating:** obtain docket/plea records, independently confirm Anthony-Gaffield's reported October plea and present case schedules, and seek comment from defense counsel and prosecutors. We have not independently reviewed the full complaint, sealed material, or raw recordings. Quote excerpts are attributed to the sources below. Do not read source numbers aloud.
